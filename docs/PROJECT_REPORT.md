## 1. Project Title
**AI-Based Sales Forecasting and Inventory Optimization System** (application name: *ForecastIQ*).

## 2. Abstract
Retailers must decide how much stock to buy before they know how much customers will order. This project builds a complete,
database-free analytics platform on the real **UCI Online Retail** dataset (541,909 transactions,
2010-12-01 to 2011-12-09). The raw lines are validated, every line is labelled (sale, return, reversed order, postage,
adjustment, ...), and the clean sales are converted into a daily product-level demand panel with leakage-safe lag, rolling,
calendar, price and history features. Three baselines and four regression models (Linear Regression, Random Forest,
HistGradientBoosting, XGBoost) are compared with chronological splits and rolling-origin hyperparameter tuning. The
selected model (HistGradientBoosting) reaches a test MAE of 21.27 units/day (WAPE 89.2%) on the untouched final
2011-10-21 – 2011-12-09 period. On the untouched test period, HistGradientBoosting did **not** beat the best baseline (Baseline: 7-day moving average): MAE 21.275 vs 21.226 (-0.2% change). This result is reported exactly as measured. A recursive engine produces 7-, 14- and 30-day forecasts.
These feed an inventory engine that computes safety stock, reorder point, target stock and order quantity from user-entered
stock and lead times. A FastAPI backend and a React dashboard make every step usable and explainable.

## 3. Introduction
Demand forecasting estimates future sales so a business can plan purchasing, warehousing and cash flow. Inventory optimisation
uses those forecasts to decide **when** to reorder and **how much**. Too little stock loses sales; too much ties up money and
space. This project combines data science (cleaning, EDA, feature engineering), machine learning (regression models evaluated
over time) and software engineering (API, dashboard, file storage, tests) into one working system.

## 4. Problem Statement
Given only historical transaction lines of an online retailer, (a) forecast each product's daily demand for the next 7, 14 or
30 days without using future information, and (b) turn the forecasts plus the user's current stock and supplier lead time into
clear, explainable reorder recommendations. All of this must run locally without any database.

## 5. Motivation
* Purchasing decisions are often based on gut feeling or simple averages.
* The UCI data is real and messy (cancellations, returns, fees, duplicates, missing IDs), which makes it a good exercise in honest data science.
* Students and examiners can verify every number, because the full pipeline is reproducible from the original Excel file.

## 10. Existing System
Small retailers typically reorder from spreadsheets: they look at last month's sales, add a margin and place an order. This approach:
* ignores weekday patterns, seasonality and trends;
* cannot quantify demand variability, so safety stock is guessed;
* mixes returns, cancellations and fees with real demand;
* has no audit trail of decisions.

An integrated platform that:

* keeps every forecast and recommendation in versioned files and exports CSV/PDF reports.

## 18. Data Preprocessing Methodology
1. **Exact duplicates** removed: 5,268 rows (identical in all 8 columns, including the minute).
2. **Every remaining row labelled** with one transaction type (priority-ordered rules):

| Type | Rows | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| SALE | 519,735 | £9,823,459.37 | Real fulfilled product sale (positive quantity and price). Used for demand and revenue. |
| REVERSED_SALE | 2,805 | £423,759.95 | Sale cancelled in full later by the same customer (same product and quantity). Excluded from demand. |
| RETURN | 8,668 | £-475,901.16 | Product line on a cancellation ('C') invoice: goods returned/cancelled. Tracked separately. |
| CANCELLATION_NON_PRODUCT | 583 | £-418,078.57 | Cancellation line for discounts, postage, fees. Not product demand. |
| STOCK_ADJUSTMENT | 1,336 | £0.00 | Negative quantity with zero price on a normal invoice (damaged, lost, stock checks). |
| ACCOUNTING_ADJUSTMENT | 3 | £-11,062.06 | Bad-debt adjustment invoices starting with 'A'. Not sales. |
| NON_PRODUCT | 2,354 | £383,829.42 | Postage, carriage, bank charges, marketplace fees, manual lines, gift vouchers. |
| ZERO_PRICE | 1,157 | £0.00 | Positive quantity with zero price: no revenue, often samples or data fixes. |
| INVALID | 0 | £0.00 | Missing/invalid timestamp, StockCode or quantity. |

3. **Cancellations are not blindly deleted.** A 'C' line that exactly reverses an earlier sale (same customer, product and quantity;
   cancellation at or after the sale) marks that sale as REVERSED_SALE: 2,805 lines. This explains the two
   famous extreme lines (80,995 and 74,215 units), which were cancelled minutes after being entered.
4. **Descriptions standardised:** one canonical upper-case description per StockCode; 1,341 missing descriptions filled.
5. **Outliers investigated, not deleted:** 2,289 unusually large sale lines were flagged and **kept**
   (Flag if quantity > product's 99th percentile AND >= 10x product median AND >= 100 units. Flagged rows are KEPT.).
6. **Forecasting target:** Gross fulfilled demand: daily sum of Quantity over SALE rows per StockCode. Returns/cancellations are tracked separately and NOT subtracted; sale lines fully reversed by a matching cancellation (REVERSED_SALE) are excluded because they were never fulfilled.
7. **Revenue definitions (reported separately):** gross fulfilled sales £9,823,459.37;
   value of cancellations/returns £-893,979.73; net after cancellations
   £9,353,239.59.

**Row accounting:** 541,909 original → 519,735 clean sale rows
(22,174 rows not used as sales; every one is counted by type above).

## 19. Feature Engineering Methodology
* **Daily demand:** units per product per day from SALE rows.
* **Products modelled:** Top 500 products by number of days with sales (ties: units sold).
* **Panel:** each product from its first to its last sale date; days without a sale inside that window = 0. No rows exist before the
  first or after the last sale, so those days are not assumed to be zero demand.
* **Closed days:** the shop never traded on weekday(s) [5] (0 = Monday, so 5 = Saturday) and was closed on
  69 dates (Christmas, bank holidays). These days stay in the series as 0, so `lag_7` is always
  the same weekday, but they are not used as training or evaluation rows.
* **Features (27):** lags 1/7/14/28, last open-day demand, rolling mean 7/14/28, rolling std 7, rolling max 7,
  calendar (day, month, year, weekday, week, weekend, month start/end), price (last known price, 28-day average price, price change %),
  history (sales frequency, days since previous sale, historical mean/std, product age). See `docs/FEATURE_DICTIONARY.md`.
* **Leakage prevention:** data sorted by product and date; `shift(1)` applied per product before every rolling or expanding
  statistic; prices only from earlier days (last known price held constant when forecasting); the same `make_features()` used for
  training and forecasting; unit tests prove that changing future demand never changes past features.
* **Model rows:** 138,700 (open days with at least 28 days of product history). Zero-demand share of targets:
  29.8%.
* **Chronological split by date:** train 2011-01-04 – 2011-08-31; validation 2011-09-01 –
  2011-10-20; test 2011-10-21 – 2011-12-09. All products on a date share the same split.

## 20. Algorithm Explanations
* **Baselines:** previous-day demand; same weekday last week; 7-day moving average. A model is only useful if it beats these.
* **Linear Regression:** predicts a weighted sum of standardised features; fast and interpretable, but cannot capture interactions.
* **Random Forest:** many decision trees, each trained on a random sample of rows and features; the average of their votes reduces overfitting.
* **HistGradientBoosting:** trees are added one after another, each correcting the remaining errors; features are binned for speed.
  A *Poisson* loss suits count-like demand.
* **XGBoost:** a regularised gradient-boosting library with row/column subsampling; offers squared-error, Poisson and Tweedie objectives.
* **Tuning:** `RandomizedSearchCV` over the listed hyperparameters with 285 dates split into
  rolling-origin folds **inside the training period only**:

| Model | CV MAE | Best hyperparameters |
|---|---|---|
| Linear Regression | — | — |
| Random Forest | 19.423 | n_estimators=300, min_samples_split=20, min_samples_leaf=3, max_features=0.5, max_depth=8 |
| HistGradientBoosting | 18.087 | l2_regularization=1.0, learning_rate=0.0502, loss=poisson, max_iter=400, max_leaf_nodes=31 |
| XGBoost | 16.804 | colsample_bytree=0.578, learning_rate=0.0229, max_depth=8, n_estimators=199, objective=reg:tweedie, subsample=0.6082, tweedie_variance_power=1.35 |

* **Loss-function design check (validation data only):**

| Loss | Validation MAE | Validation WAPE | Bias as % of mean demand |
|---|---|---|---|
| squared_error | 19.535 | 91.1% | -21.5% |
| poisson | 18.559 | 86.5% | -31.7% |
| absolute_error | 17.727 | 82.7% | -61.7% |

  Absolute-error loss is excluded from tuning: it predicts the median and under-forecasts total demand (see loss_function_experiment bias), which would cause stockouts.

## 21. Model Evaluation Methodology
* **Selection:** Lowest validation MAE among ML models (tuned with rolling-origin CV on training dates only).
* **Final test:** each model refit on train + validation, evaluated **once** on the untouched test period.
* **Production model:** Selected model refit on all model rows (train + validation + test) with the same hyperparameters. Reported test metrics come from the train+validation fit evaluated on the untouched test period.
* **Metrics:** MAE (average units of error), RMSE (punishes big misses), WAPE (total error ÷ total demand), MAPE only on days with ≥ 5
  units (because MAPE is undefined for zero demand), R² as a diagnostic, and bias (mean of predicted − actual).
* **Recursive backtest:** forecasts 30 days recursively from a fixed origin with a model that has never seen data after that origin.
* **Negative predictions:** Predictions below 0 are set to 0 because demand cannot be negative.

## 22. Inventory Optimization Methodology
Periodic-review, order-up-to policy:
* d = average daily demand from the 30-day model forecast (calendar days; closed days = 0)
* σ = standard deviation of daily demand over the last 84 days of real history (if fewer than 28 days are observed, safety stock is
  **not** computed and the result is marked low-confidence)
* Z = NormInv(service level), e.g. 95% → 1.645
* **Safety stock** SS = Z × σ × √L
* **Reorder point** ROP = d × L + SS
* **Target stock** T = d × (L + R) + SS, where the review period R defaults to 7 days
* **Order quantity** Q = max(0, T − current stock), then rounded up to MOQ and pack size, and limited by storage capacity (any
  shortfall is shown, never hidden)
* **Status (one tested function):** Critical when stock ≤ SS; Low when stock ≤ ROP; Overstocked when stock > 1.5 × T; otherwise Healthy.
* **Assumptions:** independent, identically distributed daily demand; fixed lead time; forecast demand constant over the lead time.

**ABC analysis** (cumulative revenue share, thresholds 80% / 95%): class A = 844 products
(21.6% of products) → 80.0% of revenue; B = 978
products → 15.0%; C = 2081 products → 5.0%.

## 23. Results and Discussion
### 23.1 Business findings (EDA)
* Gross fulfilled revenue £9,823,459.37 from 5,338,098 units in 19,648 orders
  (average order value £499.97), 3,903 products, 4,324 identified customers.
* **Transaction composition:** 96.8% of lines are genuine product sales. Returns, reversed orders, postage/fees and adjustments are kept out of demand so they do not distort forecasts.
* **Daily sales:** Revenue is noisy day to day (busiest day 14 Nov 2011: £111k; daily std = £17k). Average revenue per trading day is highest in December 2011 (£56k), 2.6x the lowest month (February 2011).
* **Weekly sales:** Units and revenue move together. Full weeks range from £82k to £371k revenue, so weekly planning must allow for a large swing. 1 week(s) had no trading at all (holiday closure).
* **Monthly revenue:** Best full month: November 2011 (£1.4M); weakest: February 2011 (£505k). With only ~one year of data this is a single seasonal cycle, so it is described as an observed peak in this period, not a proven yearly pattern.
* **Top 10 products by units sold:** 'POPCORN HOLDER' leads with 56,676 units. These 10 products account for 7.0% of all units; their average selling price is £1.17 per unit.
* **Top 10 products by revenue:** 'REGENCY CAKESTAND 3 TIER' leads with 170,229 GBP. These 10 products account for 8.1% of all revenue; their average selling price is £2.89 per unit.
* **Product demand variability:** 99% of products with >= 20 sale days have CV > 1 (median CV 2.87; median share of trading days with a sale 34%). Daily product demand is intermittent and bursty, so safety stock matters and day-level forecast errors will be large.
* **Country-wise revenue:** United Kingdom generates 84.7% of revenue; a log scale keeps the next markets (Netherlands, EIRE, Germany, France) visible. Demand is dominated by one market.
* **Day-of-week pattern:** Busiest weekday: Thursday; quietest: Sunday. No trading on: Saturday — the forecast treats these as closed days.
* **Hourly pattern:** Orders peak around 12:00; 98.1% of orders arrive between 08:00 and 17:59 (first hour with orders: 6:00, last: 20:00).
* **Customer purchase frequency:** 65% of identified customers ordered more than once (median 2 orders, maximum 203). Identified customers generate 84.6% of revenue.
* Demand variability: 99.2% of products with ≥ 20 sale days have CV > 1 (median CV 2.875).
  Daily demand is intermittent and bursty.

### 23.4 Explainability
The hist_gradient_boosting model bases its forecast mainly on recent averages / volatility of demand (49% of total importance). rolling: 49%; history: 44%; price: 4%; calendar: 3%; lag: 0%.

* rolling_mean_28 (30.2% of importance): Mean daily units over D-28..D-1. Shuffling it raises test MAE by 3.92 units/day; higher values push the forecast UP (rank corr +0.86).
* hist_mean_demand (24.9% of importance): Average daily units over the product's whole history up to D-1. Shuffling it raises test MAE by 3.24 units/day; higher values push the forecast UP (rank corr +0.81).
* hist_std_demand (14.9% of importance): Standard deviation of daily units over the product's history up to D-1 (demand variability). Shuffling it raises test MAE by 1.94 units/day; higher values push the forecast UP (rank corr +0.72).
* rolling_mean_7 (6.2% of importance): Mean daily units over D-7..D-1 (shift(1) then rolling 7). Shuffling it raises test MAE by 0.80 units/day; higher values push the forecast UP (rank corr +0.79).
* rolling_max_7 (4.9% of importance): Largest daily units over D-7..D-1 (recent bulk orders). Shuffling it raises test MAE by 0.63 units/day; higher values push the forecast UP (rank corr +0.75).

### 23.5 Discussion
* Daily SKU-level wholesale demand is extremely noisy: most days are small, with occasional bulk orders of hundreds of units. Even the
  best models have a WAPE near 89%. That is typical for daily intermittent demand, and it is why the inventory
  engine adds safety stock based on measured variability.
* The 7-day moving average is a strong benchmark. On validation the ML models improved on it (best validation MAE
  18.42 vs 21.15). On the later test period the gap shrank or reversed, which shows
  how much results vary between periods. Model selection never looked at the test period, so the test numbers are an honest estimate.
* Recursive forecasts feed predictions back as inputs. Their accuracy depends on the period: compare the two origins above.
* Recent averages and long-run product behaviour carry most of the signal (rolling 49%, history 44%, price 4%, calendar 3%, lag 0%).

## 24. Limitations
1. No stock-on-hand, lead time or stockout data in UCI: inventory outputs depend on user inputs and are recommendations.
2. Only about one year of data: yearly seasonality cannot be learned or proven.
3. Daily product demand is highly intermittent; forecast errors are large at day level.
4. Only the 500 products with the longest history are forecast; others have too little data.
5. Future public holidays are unknown to the model (only Saturday closures are applied).
6. Safety stock assumes independent daily demand and a fixed lead time.
7. No prediction intervals are shown, because no validated uncertainty method was implemented.
8. Single-user local application; files are locked only within one backend process.

## 25. Future Scope
* Weekly-level or hierarchical forecasts (more stable than daily), and intermittent-demand methods (Croston/TSB).
* Quantile or conformal prediction intervals → service-level-driven safety stock from forecast errors.
* Holiday and promotion calendars; price-elasticity features.
* Lead-time variability in safety stock; multi-echelon inventory.
* Real ERP/stock integration; authentication and multi-user locking; Docker deployment.
* Automatic retraining when new data arrives, and drift monitoring.

## 26. Conclusion
The project delivers a working end-to-end system. Real, messy transactions are validated and cleaned with fully traceable rules,
then turned into leakage-safe features. Several models are compared honestly against baselines with chronological evaluation,
explained, and used for recursive forecasts and documented inventory recommendations. Everything is reachable through a modern
dashboard and a REST API, with no database. The most important lesson is methodological: baselines, time-aware validation and
honest reporting matter as much as the choice of algorithm.
